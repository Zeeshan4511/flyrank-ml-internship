Abstract

Question: Can historical search-performance signals be used to identify content that is likely to experience substantial future declines in search impressions and therefore deserves refresh review? Method: I aggregated Google Search Console performance data into monthly content-level features, defined a refresh opportunity as a future decline of at least 50% in average monthly impressions, and compared a historical-trend baseline with Logistic Regression and Random Forest models using a client-grouped train/test split. Result: Random Forest achieved an Average Precision of 0.7436 and ROC-AUC of 0.7748, outperforming the historical-trend baseline at the overall ranking level, although the simple baseline remained slightly stronger among the very top-ranked items. Validation: On the held-out test set, the Random Forest achieved 82.73% precision in the top 15% of ranked opportunities compared with a 45.64% overall opportunity rate, representing approximately 1.81× lift. Conclusion: The resulting ranking can support a practical content-review workflow by concentrating likely refresh opportunities near the top, while remaining a directional decision-support tool rather than evidence that refreshing content will cause search visibility to recover.

1. Introduction / Problem Statement

The Content Prioritisation Problem

Search performance changes over time. For a content team with many pages, reviewing every page with the same level of attention is inefficient.

The practical challenge is therefore not simply identifying whether content is performing well or poorly. The more useful decision is determining which content should receive attention first.

Capstone decision problem

This project asks whether historical search-performance signals can be transformed into a ranked queue of content that deserves refresh or review.

The workflow connects:

Historical Data → Features → Model → Validation → Ranked Opportunity → Human Review

Research Question

Can historical search-performance signals identify content that subsequently experiences substantial declines in search impressions, allowing those pages to be prioritised for human refresh review?

Decision Supported

The intended decision is content refresh prioritisation.

Instead of treating every page equally, the scoring system ranks content according to historical patterns associated with later search-performance deterioration.

A higher score means that the content has stronger model-associated evidence of being a refresh opportunity. The score is intended to guide human review rather than automatically trigger a content change.

Aggregation

Daily search-performance records were aggregated to monthly content-client observations.

Impressions and clicks were summed, while average search position was calculated across the available monthly records. Click-through rate was derived from clicks and impressions.

Eligibility Rules

  • Content-client combinations required at least 3 historical months of observations.
  • Only observations with all 3 future months available were retained for the final outcome calculation.
  • Rows where the historical click-change feature could not be calculated because the starting click count was zero were excluded from the final model.

3. Methodology

Feature Engineering

Historical monthly performance was converted into a set of content-level features representing volume, efficiency, position, and historical movement.

Volume Signals

  • Total impressions
  • Total clicks
  • First impressions
  • Last impressions

Search Signals

  • Average position
  • First position
  • Last position
  • Average CTR

Trend Signals

  • Historical impression change
  • Historical position change

History Signals

  • Months observed
  • First clicks
  • Last clicks

The final feature matrix contained 13 predictive features.

Refresh Opportunity Label

The target was designed around a practical content deterioration event.

A content observation is a Refresh Opportunity when its future average monthly impressions decline by at least 50% compared with its historical average monthly impressions.
Target Meaning Count Share
0 Not a Refresh Opportunity 61,555 71.98%
1 Refresh Opportunity 23,959 28.02%

Baseline

A simple historical-trend baseline was created before evaluating machine-learning models.

The baseline ranked content using the inverse of historical impression change. In simple terms, pages showing stronger historical deterioration were ranked higher.

This baseline provides an important benchmark because a machine-learning model should demonstrate useful improvement over a simple, understandable heuristic.

Machine-Learning Models

Two classification models were evaluated:

  1. Logistic Regression — used as a simple machine-learning baseline.
  2. Random Forest — used as the primary non-linear model.

Validation Design

A client-grouped train/test split was used instead of randomly splitting individual observations.

77,942 Training observations
7,572 Test observations
20 Training clients
6 Test clients
Grouped validation: There was 0 client overlap between the training and testing groups. This reduces the risk that the model's reported performance is driven simply by seeing observations from the same client in both sets.

Observed Class Shift

The grouped split produced a meaningful difference in opportunity prevalence between training and testing clients.

  • Training refresh-opportunity rate: 26.31%
  • Test refresh-opportunity rate: 45.64%

This distribution shift was retained and reported rather than changing the split simply to make the class percentages more similar.

Leakage Check

Future outcome columns were explicitly checked against the final model feature list.

PASS — No future outcome columns were included in the model features.

The April–June 2026 future window was used to define the outcome, but those future outcome variables were not used as predictors.

Overall Ranking Performance

Random Forest achieved the strongest overall ranking performance.

Average Precision increased from 0.5097 for the historical-trend baseline to 0.7436 for Random Forest.

ROC-AUC increased from 0.6744 to 0.7748.

Important baseline finding: The simple historical-trend baseline remained slightly stronger at the very top of the ranking, achieving 98.0% Precision@100 and 94.8% Precision@500. Random Forest achieved 97.0% and 89.4% respectively.

This shows that machine learning added value to overall ranking performance, but did not dominate the simple trend heuristic at every ranking depth.

6. Limitations & Honest Framing

Retrospective Design

The analysis uses a historical period to construct features and a later period to define the outcome. The final ranking is therefore retrospective rather than a live production forecast.

Client Distribution Shift

The grouped test set had a higher refresh-opportunity rate than the training set. This affects how the validation results should be interpreted.

Baseline Strength

The simple historical trend baseline performed extremely well at the very top of the ranking.

Unobserved Causes

Impression changes can have many causes that are not represented in the available feature set.

Full-Data Ranking Caveat

After validation, the Random Forest was fitted on the complete modeling dataset to produce the final ranked recommendation queue.

Important: The full-data opportunity scores are not independent out-of-sample predictions because the final model was trained using all available retrospective labeled observations. The held-out client-group test results are therefore the primary evidence used to evaluate model performance.

What This Work Cannot Claim

  • It cannot prove Google's ranking algorithm or ranking mechanism.
  • It cannot prove that refreshing a page will cause its impressions to recover.
  • It cannot establish causal relationships between the features and search-performance changes.
  • It cannot guarantee future performance for a specific content item.
  • It cannot replace human content strategy or editorial judgement.

Supported Claim

Historical search-performance signals can be used to build a ranking that concentrates subsequently observed refresh opportunities toward the top of the queue. The result is best used as a directional decision-support tool for prioritising human content review.

7. Ranked Recommendations / Action Playbook

The final Random Forest opportunity scores were converted into percentile-based priority groups.

Priority Score Segment Content Count Recommended Action
High Priority Top 15% 12,827 Refresh
Review Next 25% 21,379 Review
Monitor Bottom 60% 51,308 Monitor

01 — High Priority: Refresh

The highest-scoring 15% should receive the first round of human content review.

Recommended checks:
  • Review content freshness.
  • Check search-intent alignment.
  • Identify outdated information.
  • Check topical coverage and completeness.
  • Review internal linking opportunities.
  • Prioritise pages with meaningful historical visibility.
Reason signals:
HISTORICAL_DECLINE POSITION_SIGNAL VISIBILITY_SIGNAL CTR_SIGNAL

02 — Review: Manual Assessment

The next 25% should enter a secondary review queue.

  • Check whether the observed decline is meaningful.
  • Consider seasonality and demand changes.
  • Review content relevance and freshness.
  • Decide whether refresh or monitoring is appropriate.

03 — Monitor: Continue Tracking

The bottom 60% does not receive an immediate refresh recommendation from this scoring system.

  • Continue monitoring performance.
  • Recalculate the ranking during the next review cycle.
  • Escalate pages if deterioration becomes stronger.

Why Percentile-Based Priorities?

The final action groups use percentile-based thresholds rather than arbitrary opportunity-score cutoffs.

This creates a practical queue where approximately 15% of observations receive the highest priority, 25% receive a secondary review, and the remaining 60% remain under monitoring.

Human Review Principle

Model ranking → Human review → Content decision

The score should help decide where to look first. It should not automatically decide what content must be changed.

The capstone was developed as a repeatable data-analysis and machine-learning workflow using Python, Pandas, scikit-learn, DuckDB, and the provided Hugging Face warehouse workflow.

Analysis Workflow

  1. Load the FlyRank warehouse release.
  2. Inspect the available performance tables and date range.
  3. Aggregate daily search-performance records to monthly content-client observations.
  4. Construct historical features from November 2025 through March 2026.
  5. Define the April–June 2026 future outcome.
  6. Apply the historical and future eligibility rules.
  7. Build and evaluate the historical-trend baseline.
  8. Train Logistic Regression and Random Forest models.
  9. Validate using a client-grouped train/test split.
  10. Run an explicit future-data leakage check.
  11. Generate the ranked refresh opportunity queue.
  12. Convert scores into operational priority groups.

9. Conclusion

This capstone developed a content refresh opportunity scoring workflow using historical search-performance data from the FlyRank ML Internship warehouse.

The analysis defined a refresh opportunity as a future decline of at least 50% in average monthly impressions and compared a simple historical-trend baseline with Logistic Regression and Random Forest.

Random Forest achieved 0.7436 Average Precision and 0.7748 ROC-AUC on the held-out client-group test set.

The model's top 15% achieved 82.73% precision, compared with a 45.64% overall opportunity rate, producing approximately 1.81× lift.

At the same time, the historical-trend baseline remained extremely strong at the very top of the ranking. This demonstrates why simple baselines should be evaluated before assuming that a more complex model is automatically better.

The final output is best understood as a content prioritisation and decision-support system: it helps identify where a content team should look first, while leaving the final refresh decision to human review.
